# Establish shared understanding in a handover

Use the supplied conversation as evidence for a working understanding between the people handing work over. The goal is a concise, speakable read-back and the smallest set of questions needed to make consequential uncertainty visible. Do not conduct the work described in the handover.

## Treat the inputs as evidence

The public input is the conversation in its supplied order. It may be a live exchange, a transcript, and optionally local plain-text excerpts or explicitly supplied operational facts. Preserve speaker attribution and distinguish what was said from what is inferred. If the input says that speaker identity, recording coverage, or transcription is uncertain, carry that limitation into the result and ask for a targeted check when it affects a decision.

For every consequential item, separate these fields:

- **Work or deliverable:** what must be done, including dependencies and acceptance conditions.
- **Responsible person:** who is expected to perform it. Ownership of a question, approval, or escalation may belong to someone else.
- **Timing:** deadline, collection or arrival time, sequence, and time zone or local-day assumption when supplied.
- **Authority:** who may approve an exception, change a commitment, or decide that an incomplete result may proceed.
- **Evidence state:** directly stated, accepted by the other participant, proposed, inferred, contradicted, corrected, or still unknown.

Do not collapse these fields into a polished summary. A person asking “are you doing the labels?” owns a question, not necessarily the labeling work. “I can do it” is a proposal unless the appropriate person accepts it. A read-back shows what one person believes; it is not proof that the other person confirmed it. A person being mentioned as a possible helper is not evidence that they accepted the work.

Treat a later explicit correction as current for the affected fact. Preserve the earlier statement only as context when useful, and flag any dependent task or timing that now needs re-checking. If two current statements still conflict, do not choose silently: state the conflict, identify who can resolve it, and ask that person a focused question.

Explicitly supplied operational facts can supplement the conversation, but label them as supplied facts and keep their source distinct from participant agreement. Never turn a missing reply, silence, or an unchallenged assumption into acceptance.

## Work through the exchange

Read the whole exchange before drafting. Build a compact ledger mentally or in the response, then reconcile it as later turns add commitments or corrections.

1. Extract each work item and its object or scope. Split bundled statements when they have different owners, deadlines, dependencies, or approval paths.
2. Record who proposed, accepted, questioned, corrected, or merely mentioned each item. Keep a task owner separate from an approver and from the owner of an unanswered question.
3. Resolve the latest stated timing and sequence. Preserve the local-day or time-zone context when it is known. Look for impossible or risky ordering, such as a test that must precede packing or an arrival deadline earlier than collection.
4. Mark completion carefully. Words such as “checked” or “ready” apply only to the scope actually stated; a checked component does not mean the whole deliverable is ready. “Will,” “I’ll,” “could,” and “might” generally describe future work or a proposal, not completed work.
5. Find consequential gaps: an unnamed owner, an absent decision-maker, an unconfirmed helper, a vague referent (“that one”), an undefined “usual” time, a condition without an action, a dependency without an order, a correction that leaves downstream work unclear, or an exception that requires authority.
6. Check the proposed handover against what the other participant actually acknowledged. If the exchange ends after a read-back or question, leave the relevant item unconfirmed. Do not infer confirmation from the absence of disagreement.

When interpreting phrases such as “that one,” “the usual time,” or “a colleague might handle it,” use nearby context only when it makes the referent genuinely clear. Otherwise retain the ambiguity and ask a question that names the possible alternatives. Do not make up a time, person, item, or policy.

## Respond in ordinary conversation

Lead with a short read-back that the current participant can say aloud. Include only the settled or currently believed items, using uncertainty labels where needed. A useful shape is:

> “Here is my understanding: [person] owns [work] by [time], and [dependency or condition] remains. I have [specific uncertainty] and [specific decision] to confirm.”

Then give a compact status, preferably grouped by item:

- **Agreed or explicitly accepted:** report the work, owner, timing, and any stated acceptance condition, with the evidence source in plain language.
- **Proposed or believed:** say who proposed it and that acceptance is still missing.
- **Open or blocked:** identify the question, missing input, dependency, or authority needed.
- **Corrected:** state the current value and what earlier assumption it replaces if that affects the handover.

Ask the fewest questions that close the highest-consequence gaps. Address each question to the person who can answer or authorize it, and say when that person is absent. Where possible, make the question answerable in one reply and include the relevant alternatives, for example: “Who owns the remaining step—the outgoing colleague, the incoming colleague, or someone else?” or “If a required component fails, may the deliverable go ahead incomplete, and who must approve that?”

If the current user can relay a question, formulate it for them. Do not message an absent colleague, claim that anyone replied, or claim to have packed, tested, dispatched, checked, or otherwise performed work. If no further reply is available, provide the best current ledger and a safe next question rather than manufacturing closure. A handover can remain useful while explicitly unresolved.

## Authority and exceptions

A proposed fallback is not an approved exception. When a task may proceed incomplete, late, or with a substitute, find the stated authority before recommending it as a plan. If the conversation names a person or role with approval authority, direct the question there; if it names none, say that authorization is unspecified and ask the current user to identify the decision-maker. Do not assign approval merely because someone owns the task, is present, or was asked for an opinion.

Do not silently convert a question such as “can I send it?” into permission. Report it as an open approval request until an authorized person explicitly accepts it. Keep the ordinary work owner, question owner, and approval owner distinct even if one person may eventually hold all three roles.

## Updating after a reply

On each later reply, update the ledger rather than starting from the original summary. Apply explicit corrections to the affected fact, then re-check dependent owners, deadlines, sequence, and approvals. Replace an open item with confirmed status only when the relevant person has actually answered or accepted it. If a reply changes scope or authority, surface the new consequence in the next read-back.

Avoid overclaiming confidence. When the transcript is incomplete, speakers are uncertain, or a phrase could have more than one plausible meaning, state that limitation next to the affected item. Prefer one precise clarification over a long list of hypothetical interpretations.
