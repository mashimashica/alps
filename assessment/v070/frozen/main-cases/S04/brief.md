# Work out what the proof-request pilot can support

Create an Agent Skill for a facilitator helping a small business clarify requirements and assess feasibility over several conversations. A later fresh assistant will use the Skill to work with supplied local shared information. The assignment is Skill design, not building the pilot or implementing a new application.

The fictional Harbor Printworks is considering a four-week pilot for handling client proof requests. Discussion covers request intake, proof review and pickup coordination. Leah, the client-services lead, approves the desired business scope. Owen, the production lead, can commit operational effort. Mina, the technology lead, confirms what the existing tools support. Rafi facilitates the conversations and keeps the working information current. One person's preference does not authorize a commitment in another person's area.

Their public starting context includes these facts:

- Leah wants clients to know whether a proof is awaiting review, approved or ready for pickup, with less chasing by phone. She has suggested a live client-facing status page, but a suggestion is not an agreed solution.
- Mina's current capability note says existing tools support internal request forms, manually set statuses and scheduled email digests. They do not provide a public client portal, text messaging or automatic artwork validation.
- Owen can commit at most five staff hours per week to pilot administration and has offered two scheduled manual status updates per working day. No evidence yet establishes the work required by every proposed feature.
- The pilot must use existing paid tools; a new subscription is outside its approved scope. Client artwork and private contact information must not be copied into general discussion notes. Synthetic request identifiers and invented examples are sufficient here.

The participants will clarify terms, question assumptions, propose alternatives, revise earlier requests and sometimes disagree. They want the shared working information to retain the current meaning of a requirement, why it changed, what remains uncertain, and whose agreement or evidence is still needed. Feasibility should be grounded in the available capability, effort and timing facts; unmeasured effort must not become an invented estimate, and recording a proposal must not turn it into approval. The assistant can help participants find a narrower workable option or establish why a decision is premature. It is not expected to make every request feasible.

Sometimes Rafi will ask to update the shared working information after a discussion. At other times a stakeholder only wants a read-only overview across all three areas: what is agreed, what conflicts, what depends on something else, and which decisions are still open. That overview must not itself alter source information, resolve disagreements on people's behalf or silently “tidy up” records. It should be possible to revisit it after later updates without treating an old overview as authoritative.

For later use, a synthetic local bundle will provide exact paths to existing working notes, authoritative capability and resource statements, and ordered discussion excerpts. Each source's owner and whether it is editable will be explicit. Working notes may be updated when the user requests that work; authoritative statements and conversation evidence remain read-only unless the supplied permissions explicitly change. No particular note format or newly created document is required. If sources conflict or an update's intended scope is unclear, that is part of the business problem rather than permission to invent a resolution. All collaboration is represented by supplied text and local information; no real stakeholder is contacted, no external system is modified, and no new runtime is needed.
