# Recommendation to Nessa Vale

**Stop Alder's all-order second-person packing check for the next twenty full shifts, keep Birch's current no-check process, and do not expand or selectively narrow the check yet.** This is an interim operating decision for Nessa to approve; no operational change has been made.

The measured check does not fit two hard operating conditions. Excluding the separately funded five training hours, Alder used 270 recurring productive hours for 3,600 orders during the three ordinary pilot shifts, or 0.075 hour per order. A constant-rate projection requires **108 hours** for 1,440 orders, six hours over the **102-hour hard cap**. At that rate the cap supports only **1,360 orders**, while orders may not be moved, deferred, or cancelled. Alder also dispatched **42 of 3,600 orders late (1.17%)** on checked full shifts, above the **1% commitment**. The short, unusually simple August 28 shift had zero late orders on both lines, but its 600-order result is not evidence of full-shift capacity.

Stopping also has the stronger quality and cost planning case. The apparent raw improvement at Alder disappears when rates are evaluated at next period's 70% standard / 30% complex mix. The comparison-adjusted estimate is a **0.6 percentage-point disadvantage** for checking, not an improvement. That estimate is useful for planning but is not causal proof.

## Quality basis

Only mature cohorts from August 3–5 and August 17–19 are used for seven-day mispacks. August 28 has no mature orders and remains pending. Station catches are excluded because they are pre-dispatch events and recording changed on August 14.

The common-mix adjusted contrast is:

`(3.2% - 3.7%) - (2.6% - 2.5%) = -0.6 percentage point`

The sign means Alder worsened by 0.6 percentage point relative to Birch. Allocation was voluntary rather than randomized; Alder began with worse complex-order performance; the complex account pause changed both lines' mix; and a common packing-list change occurred just before the pilot. There are only three mature dates per period and 300 mature complex pilot orders per line. Parallel trends are untested, so this contrast must not be described as the causal effect of checking.

## Twenty-shift economics for Alder

For a named no-check planning baseline, apply Birch's target-mix period change to Alder's target-mix baseline: `3.2% + (2.5% - 2.6%) = 3.1%`. Compare that with Alder's checked pilot rate of 3.7%, both at the planned mix and across 28,800 orders.

| Scenario | Expected mispack rate | Expected mispacks | Expected avoidable mispack cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Stop check; parallel-change no-check scenario | 3.1% | 892.8 | $49,104 |
| Continue check; pilot association | 3.7% | 1,065.6 | $58,608 |
| Checking disadvantage | 0.6 pp | 172.8 more | **$9,504 more** |

At the observed recurring labor rates, checking adds 9.6 hours per shift relative to Alder's baseline, or 192 hours over twenty shifts. At $34 per additional productive hour, that is **$6,528**. Under this planning scenario, checking has an expected total disadvantage of **$16,032** ($9,504 error cost plus $6,528 labor cost). Its expected avoided errors are negative 172.8. Even if checking had no quality effect, it would need an improvement of about **0.412 percentage point** to offset this added labor cost at $55 per avoided mispack. Feasibility still takes precedence: a positive cost scenario could not authorize hours above the cap. The five pilot training hours are sunk and separately funded; stopping adds no next-period training.

## Operating scope and reviews

For all twenty planned shifts, the recommended scope is no second-person all-order check on either line, 1,440 orders per line at the planned 70 / 30 mix, and no more than 102 productive hours per line per shift. Do not rely on transfers, an added station, deferred volume, or extra hours. A selective check is not an implementable compromise because neither its time nor its quality and dispatch effect has been measured; scaling all-order labor by the selected order share would be unsupported. Expansion to Birch loses for the same reason and has no measured benefit.

Nessa should perform an early operating review after the first two full shifts using, for each line, actual order count and mix, total productive hours, overtime as a subset, and late-dispatch orders. The decision question is whether the no-check fallback can deliver the new 1,440-order, 70 / 30 workload within 102 hours and no more than 1% late dispatches. Keep checking off if either line misses a constraint. Because Nessa cannot relax volume or staffing constraints, any unresolved conflict must be elevated outside her stated authority before relying on the remaining-shift plan.

Perform the first outcome review after the first five shift cohorts have completed their seven-calendar-day windows. Report mature mispacks separately for standard and complex orders, standardize both lines to the actual planned mix, and retain immature cohorts as pending. Review again after all twenty cohorts mature. Nessa owns both reviews. These reviews should test whether no-check quality remains tolerable and whether a redesigned, bounded check trial is warranted; the packet supplies no universal acceptable quality threshold, so Nessa must obtain that decision criterion before approving a new quality trial.

Reconsider a check only with a separately specified design whose observed total labor can fit at or below `102 / 1,440 = 0.07083` hour per order at the planned mix, whose dispatch plan meets 1%, and whose mature target-mix quality comparison could change the decision. Recalculate the economic break-even using that design's actual incremental labor. Until those conditions are demonstrated, the operating decision remains to stop the check.
