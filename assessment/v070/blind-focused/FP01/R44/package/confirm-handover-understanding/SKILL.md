# Confirm Handover Understanding

Help colleagues reach an accurate, revisable understanding of an operational handover. Work from the conversation, optional local plain-text excerpts, and explicitly supplied operational facts. Do not require conversion into a form or fixed document.

## Build the current understanding

Preserve speaker order and treat the latest explicit correction as current while retaining enough context to say what it replaced. For each consequential item, track only what the evidence supports:

- the concrete action, object, or condition;
- the person named or accepting as work owner, and how that ownership was established;
- the deadline or sequence constraint;
- dependencies, remaining work, and exception conditions;
- who has authority to decide or approve;
- the evidence state and any source limitation.

Resolve references such as “that one,” relative or customary times, pronouns, and vague owners only when the surrounding exchange makes the referent unambiguous. Otherwise preserve the ambiguity and ask about it. Treat a supplied policy or operational fact as a constraint, not as evidence that anyone performed an action or accepted ownership.

Use evidence states that keep the distinction meaningful in ordinary language:

- **confirmed:** the relevant person explicitly accepts or confirms the item;
- **read back:** a participant repeats their understanding, but the relevant person has not confirmed it;
- **stated:** one person asserts the item, without the relevant person's confirmation;
- **proposed or asked:** a suggestion, request, offer, or question awaits an answer;
- **unresolved:** the exchange leaves a consequential detail missing or conflicting;
- **superseded:** a later correction replaces the earlier version;
- **completed:** use only when the conversation supplies evidence that the work actually occurred.

Do not promote a suggestion into acceptance, an assignment into completion, or a read-back into mutual confirmation. A person who volunteers to chase an answer owns that follow-up question; they do not thereby own the underlying work. Silence, lack of objection, or a message sent to an absent person does not establish agreement.

Compare the participants' expressed understandings item by item. Surface differing owners, times, scope, or exception paths even when each version sounds plausible on its own.

## Focus the confirmation exchange

First express the current belief compactly in the user's natural medium, usually as a short spoken read-back or message draft. Separate what appears settled from what still needs confirmation. Mention replaced details only when they prevent someone from acting on a stale instruction.

Then raise the smallest set of consequential questions needed to act safely. Prioritize mismatches or gaps involving:

1. who will do the work;
2. when it must be done or handed over;
3. what remains, including dependencies and failure paths;
4. whether the proposed action is allowed and who can approve an exception.

Address each question to a person who can confirm the fact, accept the work, or exercise the required authority. If that person is absent, formulate the question and mark the item pending; do not claim to contact them. When an authority constraint blocks a proposed fallback, say that plainly and request a decision from the authorized person rather than endorsing the fallback.

Prefer conversational wording over an audit-style report. A useful pattern is:

> My current understanding is [settled items]. I still need confirmation on [specific gaps]. [Named person], can you confirm [decision or ownership] by [relevant point]?

Adapt or omit the pattern when the user asks for a structured summary. Do not bury uncertainty in polished prose.

## Incorporate replies and corrections

When more conversation arrives, append it as later evidence and rebuild the current understanding. Replace superseded facts, update evidence states only when the reply supports the change, and surface any new conflict introduced by the reply. A bare acknowledgment confirms only what it clearly refers to; ask when its scope is ambiguous.

Continue until the consequential items are confirmed or the available exchange ends. If there is no further reply, remain useful: provide the best provisional understanding, list the few open items and their decision owners, and state what cannot yet be treated as agreed. Never manufacture closure.

## Respect source and action limits

Call out uncertain speaker identity, missing portions, interruptions, unclear recording coverage, or conflicting excerpts when they could change ownership, timing, authority, or remaining work. Ask for the smallest missing excerpt or clarification that would resolve the issue.

Do not claim to contact absent colleagues, update a task system, perform handover work, obtain approval, or complete an operational action. You may draft what the current user could say next. Keep later replies revisable and distinguish synthetic follow-up from real-world action.
