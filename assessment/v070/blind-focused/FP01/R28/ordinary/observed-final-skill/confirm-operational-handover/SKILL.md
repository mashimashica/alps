# Confirm an Operational Handover

Help the user state what they believe the handover means and obtain the right confirmation. Work from the conversation, any local plain-text excerpts, and explicitly supplied operational facts. Preserve the limits of that evidence.

Do not require the user to first turn the exchange into a form. A short spoken response may be the best result. Use a compact table or structured list only when the handover is dense enough to benefit from one.

## Reconstruct the current understanding

Read speaker-attributed material in order. Track each operational item separately, including:

- the work or outcome;
- who will perform it, if anyone has accepted it;
- deadlines, handoff points, and sequencing;
- remaining work, dependencies, contingencies, and blockers;
- decisions or exceptions and who has authority to make them;
- the speaker and wording that support each interpretation;
- later corrections that supersede earlier statements.

Do not flatten different kinds of evidence into one generic status. Keep these dimensions distinct when they matter:

**Agreement**

- **Proposed:** suggested, requested, conditional, or attributed to someone who has not accepted.
- **Accepted:** the person who would own the work explicitly takes it on.
- **Aligned:** the relevant people independently state the same understanding. This shows a match in the available exchange, but does not prove that one person confirmed the other's read-back.
- **Confirmed:** an appropriate person explicitly affirms the specific read-back or interpretation.
- **Unresolved or disputed:** a question, mismatch, ambiguity, or disagreement remains.
- **Superseded:** a later correction replaces the earlier value within the correction's scope.

**Execution**

- planned, reported in progress, reported complete, or independently verified;
- unknown when the exchange does not say.

A commitment does not prove execution. Treat a completion statement as reported unless the supplied evidence actually verifies it.

**Authority**

- distinguish the person doing work, the person coordinating a question, the source of a fact, and the person authorized to approve an exception;
- treat an explicit operational rule as a constraint, even when nobody in the exchange repeats it;
- never turn silence or a lower-authority person's opinion into approval.

Use these labels selectively. In ordinary conversation, plain phrases such as “Eli has taken that on,” “Sam has not accepted yet,” or “this still needs Maya's approval” are usually clearer.

## Interpret cautiously

Resolve pronouns and phrases such as “that one,” “the usual time,” or “they'll handle it” only when the nearby exchange supports one reading. Otherwise name the ambiguity and the consequence of getting it wrong. Do not silently guess.

Apply corrections prospectively to the exact fact corrected. Retain enough trace of the earlier value to explain a mismatch, but present the corrected value as current. If the scope of a correction is unclear, ask.

Observe the following evidence rules:

- Asking a question or offering to check something does not assign the underlying work to the questioner.
- “I asked,” “might,” “could,” and similar wording do not show that the named person accepted.
- A suggestion, request, or contingency is not an agreement.
- A person's repetition of a detail shows their understanding; it is not evidence that the other person confirmed the repetition.
- Matching statements can establish observed alignment without inventing a confirmation event.
- No reply does not mean yes.
- A plan or promised action is not completed work.
- Later evidence may revise any provisional understanding.

If speaker identity, transcript coverage, recording quality, timing, or ordering is uncertain, state how that limits attribution or confidence. Do not infer missing turns.

## Find the gaps that matter

Prioritize questions by operational consequence, not by how easy they are to fill:

1. required approval, safety, compliance, or a rule that blocks action;
2. imminent deadlines, handoff points, missing owners, and dependencies that could prevent completion;
3. conflicting or vague details that would change what someone does;
4. useful context that does not affect the immediate handover.

Avoid interrogating the user about every blank field. Ask only questions that could change an action, owner, time, sequence, or authorized decision. When several gaps can be confirmed by the same person, combine them naturally.

Direct each question to an appropriate source:

- the proposed worker must accept ownership;
- a knowledgeable source should resolve a factual ambiguity;
- the person who issued or changed a deadline should resolve a deadline conflict when the record does not;
- only the stated decision-maker may approve an exception.

If that person is absent, identify the pending question and who must answer it. You may draft wording for the user to say or send, but do not claim to contact anyone. Do not assign the absent person merely because someone else nominated them.

## Conduct the confirmation loop

Offer a concise read-back in the user's natural voice. Separate what appears settled from what remains open, and attach uncertainty to the affected point rather than weakening the whole summary. For example:

> Here is what I believe we have agreed: [accepted work, owner, and time]. I still have [specific unresolved point]. [Appropriate person], can you confirm [decision or fact]?

Adapt the wording to the setting. Do not announce evidence-taxonomy labels in a spoken response unless they help the user.

When a reply arrives:

1. attribute the reply to its speaker and check whether that person can confirm the relevant point;
2. update only the facts the reply addresses;
3. call out any correction and what it replaced;
4. distinguish acceptance, confirmation, and a new proposal;
5. give the revised read-back and ask only the remaining consequential questions.

An explicit affirmation such as “yes,” “correct,” or an equivalent response confirms only the read-back it clearly refers to. If an interruption, intervening topic, bundled question, or vague response makes that scope uncertain, ask a narrower question.

If there is no further reply, still provide a useful provisional snapshot. State what the evidence supports, what remains pending, who must confirm it, and which action is blocked by missing authority. Do not manufacture closure. Where an explicit rule requires approval, uncertainty means the exception remains unapproved.

## Shape the response

For a small handover, prefer:

1. a brief first-person read-back;
2. one or two prioritized confirmation questions;
3. a short pending note if an absent person or authority-holder must respond.

For a larger handover, a compact ledger may use columns such as item, current understanding, owner, timing, evidence state, and open confirmation. Follow it with natural wording the user can actually say.

Do not imply that you performed operational work, dispatched anything, updated a task system, or sent a message. Do not claim that a polished summary itself constitutes agreement.

## Check before responding

Ensure that:

- every consequential owner and deadline is either grounded or visibly open;
- questions, coordination, work ownership, and approval authority remain distinct;
- corrections replace only what they actually correct;
- proposals, read-backs, acceptance, confirmation, execution, and verification are not conflated;
- absent people and silence are not treated as consent;
- the response remains useful if nobody replies; and
- a later correction can update the snapshot without rewriting unsupported history.
